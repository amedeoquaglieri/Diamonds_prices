Distributions

Both carat and price are right-skewed: most stones are small and cheap, with a long tail of larger, pricier ones.

Carats cluster just under round numbers

Zooming into 0.28–1.05 carat shows sharp spikes right before 0.30, 0.70, 0.90 and 1.00 carat — and a drop just after. Cutters shave weight to land just under a round-number threshold, since crossing it (e.g. 0.99 → 1.00 ct) jumps the price bracket buyers expect to pay. This is market behavior, not a data error.

Carat is the dominant price driver

Price rises with carat as a power law rather than a straight line (price ≈ carat1.68): correlation is 0.92 on raw values but 0.97 in log–log space. Color shows clarity (I1 lightest/worst → IF darkest/best) — note how, at a fixed carat, higher clarity still commands a visibly higher price.

Correlation overview

Carat and the physical size dimensions (x/y/z) are almost interchangeable (r ≥ 0.97 with each other) and all correlate strongly with price. Depth and table — the cut-proportion measurements — barely relate to price at all.

The 4Cs confound: raw averages point the wrong way

Averaged over the whole dataset, "better" clarity and color actually look cheaper, and "Ideal" cut looks cheapest of all. That's not a quality discount — it's carat sneaking in as a confounder: the highest-clarity, best-color, best-cut stones in this dataset skew smaller (jewelers cut their finest rough into smaller, cleaner stones), and carat swamps everything else on price.

Hold carat fixed, and quality shows up as expected

Restricting to stones between 0.99 and 1.01 carat (n=3,807) removes the size confound. Now price rises cleanly with clarity and color, and cut shows a real (if noisier) premium for Ideal/Very Good over Fair. This is the honest way to read the 4Cs: their effect is real, but only visible once carat is controlled for.

Takeaways

  • Carat dominates price, non-linearly (roughly price ∝ carat1.68); size dimensions (x/y/z) carry almost the same signal as carat itself, so a model would only need one of the two groups.
  • Cut, color and clarity have real but secondary effects that are invisible in raw averages and only appear once carat is held constant — any model or chart comparing "the 4Cs" needs to control for carat first, or it will show the opposite of the true effect.
  • Depth and table (cut-proportion) barely correlate with price directly, but depth and table correlate with each other (r=−0.30), suggesting they matter jointly to a stone's cut grade rather than individually to price.
  • Carat values bunch just under round numbers (0.99, 0.30, 0.70…), a cutting/market-pricing artifact worth keeping in mind for any carat-based binning or modeling.
  • Data isn't fully clean: 20 rows with a zero dimension, a handful of rows with obvious decimal-point typos in y/z, and 289 rows involved in exact duplicates should be handled before any modeling.
