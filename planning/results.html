The confound, confirmed without a model

Spearman correlation between grade and price-per-carat, computed separately within each carat band (bars) versus pooled across all carat sizes (dashed line). Pooled, the relationship is confounded to near zero — the EDA's "better quality looks cheaper" finding. Within any single band it's positive throughout, confirming the model's direction by a method that doesn't fit anything.

Error by carat size

RMSE for the final model, computed separately within each carat band. Error grows with band size because a fixed percentage error is a bigger dollar error on a bigger stone, not because the model fits large stones worse in relative terms — and the residual shows essentially no leftover correlation with carat (0.06 Spearman), confirming the log-carat term has captured the size effect.

Takeaways

  • LightGBM on carat + ordinal grades is the final model: RMSE $529, R² 0.991, beating the best linear model (OLS, one-hot, RMSE $774) by $245.
  • Every check agrees on direction once carat is controlled for: the OLS coefficients, the tree's partial dependence, and the model-free carat-bucketed correlation all show cut/color/clarity raising price — three independent methods converging on the same answer that raw averages get backwards.
  • Cut is the weakest and noisiest of the 4Cs throughout: the smallest OLS coefficient, the flattest partial-dependence curve, and the only grade with non-monotonic cell means in the carat-bucketed check.
  • The model has no leftover size bias: residual–carat correlation is 0.06, confirming log(carat) fully captures the size effect this whole project is about.
  • One-hot beats ordinal for every linear model, a finding from the final comparison that didn't change the model choice (LightGBM wins either way) but explains why the readable OLS baseline (ordinal, for its single coefficient per grade) isn't also the most accurate linear variant.
